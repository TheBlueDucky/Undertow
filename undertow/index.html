How to play

Five minutes of reading and you will know everything. The whole game is two ideas: pieces shove, pieces drag, and the floor has holes in it.

The one thing to understand

There is no capturing in Undertow. You cannot land on an enemy piece and remove it. The only way anything leaves the board is by being moved onto a void — one of the five holes in the middle of the board. Every attack in the game is an attempt to line an enemy up with a hole and then give them a shove or a tug.

You win the moment the enemy Trident goes in.

Taking a turn

  1. Pick a piece. Click any of your own pieces. The board lights up with everything it can do.
  2. Read the marks. A small dot is a quiet move to an empty square. A ring around an enemy is an attack. A red ring means that attack kills — someone is going into a hole.
  3. Click your choice. That's the whole turn. One action each, then it's the other player's move.

Pushing

Your Sword, Bow, Spear and Trident push. A push drives the target away from you, and then you move into the square it just left. That happens whether or not the push killed anything, so pushing is also how you advance into contested ground.

Three things stop a push, and if none of them can happen the push simply isn't a legal move:

  • A wall. The edge of the board is solid. A piece with its back against the rim cannot be pushed at all — it is the safest place on the board.
  • A stack. You can shove a target with one piece behind it, and the whole pair slides. Three in a row is immovable, so a wall of your own pieces is real protection.
  • A hole. The target stops there permanently, which is the entire point.

Pulling

Your Scythe, Rod and Trident pull. A pull drags the target one square toward you and you don't move at all. You cannot pull a piece that's already next to you — there's nowhere for it to go.

The critical detail: a pull scan reaches straight across holes. A Rod standing on the lip of a void can reach over it and drag an enemy on the far side directly in. This is the single most dangerous pattern in the game and it is what the Rod exists to do.

Reading the danger

Your Trident is the strongest piece on the board — it moves three squares in any direction and attacks both ways. That makes it very tempting to use, and using it is how most people lose. Every square it stands on is a square that can be pushed or pulled.

When your Trident can be taken on the opponent's very next turn, it pulses red. That warning is a courtesy, not a rule — there is no check, no forced escape, and nothing stops you from walking straight into a hole.

Three ways a game ends

  • A Trident falls. Its owner loses, instantly.
  • Threefold repetition. If the same position occurs three times, the game stops and whoever has more pieces wins. Dead even is a draw — so if you're behind on material, you are the one who has to break the loop.
  • No legal moves. If it's your turn and you cannot do anything at all, you lose.

Four things worth knowing early

Rods want to sit next to holes

A Rod on a void's lip threatens everything on the far side of that hole, all the way down the line. Get one there early and your opponent has to spend moves avoiding a whole rank.

Back to the wall is safe

Nothing can be pushed into the rim. Retreating to the edge genuinely protects a piece — but it also takes it out of the fight.

Stacks of three don't move

Three pieces in a line along the push direction cannot be shoved at all. Building one in front of a hole shuts that hole down.

The Spear hits from range 2

It cannot touch anything adjacent, and it drives its target two full squares. It's the only piece that can reach past the front line — and the only one that can feed two pieces into one hole in a single turn.

Rules

The precise version. Where this page and the How to Play page seem to disagree, this one is what the game actually implements.

1. Board and setup

The board is 7×7. Files are lettered a–g left to right; ranks are numbered 1–7 from Amber's side.

Five squares are voids — holes in the floor. Voids are always confined to ranks 3–5, so no player's starting position is ever compromised. d4, the centre square, is always a void; the other four form two pairs placed symmetrically under a 180° rotation, so the two halves of the board are always mirror images. The layout is regenerated at random for every game, and rejected and redrawn if it would cut the board into disconnected regions.

Each player has fourteen pieces:

Amber occupies ranks 1–2 and always moves first. Violet occupies ranks 6–7.

2. Turns

On your turn you take exactly one action with exactly one piece: either a move to an empty square, or an attack on an enemy piece. Passing is not allowed. No piece may ever move onto, or slide through, a void or an occupied square.

3. Voids

A void is a permanent hole. Any piece that is displaced onto a void is removed from the game. Voids never fill up — a hole that has swallowed three pieces is exactly as dangerous as it was at the start. Voids block movement and block push paths, but they do not block pull scans.

4. Pushing

A push scans outward from the attacker in one direction. Every square between the attacker and its target must be empty; a void or any piece stops the scan short. The first enemy piece found within the piece's range is the target.

The chain is the target plus any pieces standing in an unbroken line directly behind it, up to a maximum of two pieces total. If three or more pieces are stacked in the push direction, the push is illegal.

The chain is then driven n squares away from the attacker, one step at a time. On each step:

  • If the square ahead of the leading piece is off the board, the push stops there. Remaining steps are forfeited.
  • If it is a void, the leading piece falls in and is removed, and every piece behind it advances one square into the space freed up.
  • Otherwise every piece in the chain advances one square.

If nothing can move even one square, the push is not a legal move. Otherwise the attacker finishes by advancing into the square the target originally occupied — one square for a contact attacker, two for the Spear.

Consequence worth noting: a two-piece chain being driven two squares into a hole directly ahead of it loses both pieces, because the second step feeds the survivor into the same void.

5. Pulling

A pull scans outward from the attacker in one direction, passing over both empty squares and voids. The first piece the scan meets is the target. If that piece is your own, or is directly adjacent to you, there is no pull.

The target is dragged one square toward the attacker. That square is necessarily empty or a void; if it is a void, the target is removed. The puller does not move, and pulls never chain.

When the Trident's push of one and push of two would produce different outcomes, the game asks which you meant.

7. Winning

  1. Trident. A player whose Trident is removed loses immediately. In the rare case where a single push removes both Tridents at once, the player who made that move loses.
  2. Repetition. When the same position — the same pieces on the same squares with the same player to move — occurs for the third time, the game ends. The player with more pieces on the board wins; an exact tie is a draw.
  3. No legal moves. A player with no legal action available on their turn loses.
  4. Resignation. Available at any time.

8. Notation

Moves are recorded as a piece letter, the origin square, a connector, and the target square. Because Sword, Scythe and Spear all begin with the same letter, the letters are Sword, sCythe, sPear, Bow, Rod, Trident.

- is a quiet move, > a push, < a pull. A trailing digit is a push distance greater than one; each * is a piece that went into a void.

So Rc3<c5* is a Rod on c3 pulling the piece on c5 into a hole, and Pc2>c4 2** is a Spear driving a pair two squares and losing both to the same void.

About

Undertow is an original two-player abstract strategy game. This is the complete implementation — engine, interface and networking — running entirely in your browser.

How the multiplayer works

There is no game server. When you open a room, your browser registers a short code with a public WebRTC matchmaking broker, which does nothing but introduce the two of you. From then on, moves travel directly between the two browsers. No account, no database, no record of the game.

Neither browser trusts the other. A move arrives as a short reference, not as a board state, and the receiving side regenerates the full list of legal moves for the current position and looks for a match. A move that isn't in that list is rejected and the connection is dropped, so a modified client cannot force an illegal position.

Design notes

The rule that shapes everything else is that pushes always end with the attacker taking the square its target vacated. That makes a push simultaneously an attack and an advance, which is why the board opens up quickly despite starting 57% full.

The second is that pull scans ignore voids while push scans are blocked by them. Holes are cover against half the army and an open lane for the other half, so the same square is safe or lethal depending on what is looking at it.

Playing offline

Everything except online play works with no internet connection at all — the rules, the board, the settings and the themes are entirely local. Only the initial handshake between two players needs the network.

Verifying the rules

The rule engine has its own test suite covering push chains, wall blocks, void interactions, the pull-across-a-hole case, all three endings and a randomised full-game fuzz run. Open tests.html to run it.
