How to play

Five minutes of reading and you will know everything. The whole game is two ideas: pieces shove, pieces drag, and the floor has holes in it.

The one thing to understand

There is no capturing in Undertow. You cannot land on an enemy piece and remove it. The only way anything leaves the board is by being moved onto a void — one of the four holes in the middle of the board. Every attack in the game is an attempt to line an enemy up with a hole and then give them a shove or a tug.

You win the moment every other Trident has gone in.

Taking a turn

  1. Pick a piece. Click any of your own pieces. The board lights up with everything it can do.
  2. Read the marks. A small dot is a quiet move to an empty square. A ring around an enemy is an attack. A red ring means that attack kills — someone is going into a hole. An orange square is an enemy barrier you can hit.
  3. Click your choice. That's the whole turn. One action each, then it's the next player's move.

Pushing

Your Sword, Bow, Spear and Trident push. A push drives the target away from you, and then you move into the square it just left. That happens whether or not the push killed anything, so pushing is also how you advance into contested ground.

Three things stop a push, and if none of them can happen the push simply isn't a legal move:

  • A wall. The edge of the board is solid. A piece with its back against the rim cannot be pushed at all — it is the safest place on the board.
  • A stack. You can shove a target with one piece behind it, and the whole pair slides. Three in a row is immovable, so a wall of your own pieces is real protection.
  • A hole. The target stops there permanently, which is the entire point.

Pulling

Your Scythe, Rod and Trident pull. A pull drags the target one square toward you and you don't move at all. You cannot pull a piece that's already next to you — there's nowhere for it to go.

The critical detail: a pull scan reaches straight across holes. A Rod standing on the lip of a void can reach over it and drag an enemy on the far side directly in. This is the single most dangerous pattern in the game and it is what the Rod exists to do.

Your barrier

Each player carries one barrier. Spending a whole turn drops it on any empty square, anywhere on the board — so you can wall off a hole at exactly the moment it matters, or seal a lane your opponent was counting on.

A barrier is a genuine wall: nothing moves onto it, no push travels through it, and no pull reaches past it — not even yours. It starts with three health. Any enemy piece that could have attacked through that square can spend its turn hitting the barrier instead, taking one health off. Three hits and it's gone, and the lane reopens.

Ranged pieces hit it at range: a Bow can chip a barrier from across the board, a Spear only from exactly two squares, a Sword only from right next to it.

Reading the danger

Your Trident is the strongest piece on the board — it moves three squares in any direction and attacks both ways. That makes it very tempting to use, and using it is how most people lose. Every square it stands on is a square that can be pushed or pulled.

When your Trident can be taken on someone's very next turn, it pulses red. That warning is a courtesy, not a rule — there is no check, no forced escape, and nothing stops you from walking straight into a hole.

Four-player games

Four players take one side each, and turns go around the board: Amber, Jade, Violet, Coral. Everyone who isn't you is a target — you can push, pull and strike any of them.

When someone's Trident falls they are out, but their army stays on the board. It turns grey, never moves again, and becomes scenery for everyone else: still blocking lanes, still shovable, still very much able to be dropped into a hole. The last player with a Trident wins.

Ways a game ends

  • Tridents fall. Lose yours and you're out. Last one standing wins.
  • Threefold repetition. If the same position occurs three times, the game stops and whoever has the most pieces wins. Dead even is a draw — so if you're behind on material, you are the one who has to break the loop.
  • No legal moves. If it's your turn and you cannot do anything at all, you're out.

Things worth knowing early

Rods want to sit next to holes

A Rod on a void's lip threatens everything on the far side of that hole, all the way down the line. Get one there early and your opponent has to spend moves avoiding a whole rank.

Back to the wall is safe

Nothing can be pushed into the rim. Retreating to the edge genuinely protects a piece — but it also takes it out of the fight, and on an 11×11 board the edge is a long way from anywhere.

Stacks of three don't move

Three pieces in a line along the push direction cannot be shoved at all. Building one in front of a hole shuts that hole down without spending your barrier.

The barrier is a tempo weapon

Costing a turn to place and three turns to remove, a barrier trades one of your moves for three of theirs — but only if they actually need that square. Dropped somewhere they can ignore, it's just a wasted turn.

The Spear hits from range 2

It cannot touch anything adjacent, and it drives its target two full squares. It's the only piece that can feed two pieces into one hole in a single turn.

In four-player, don't go first

Whoever commits to an attack first is usually the one who gets punished by the other two. Dead armies stay on the board as cover — sometimes the corpse is worth more than the kill.

Rules

The precise version. Where this page and the How to Play page seem to disagree, this one is what the game actually implements.

1. Board and setup

The board is 11×11. Files are lettered a–k; ranks are numbered 1–11 from Amber's side.

Four squares are voids — holes in the floor. They are confined to the central 7×7 core, which is clear of every player's home ground, and they always form a single orbit under a 90° rotation about the centre square. Two-player fairness only needs 180° symmetry, but four-player fairness needs 90°, and under a quarter turn every non-central square has an orbit of exactly four — which is why the count is four and not three. The layout is redrawn at random every game, and rejected if it would cut the board into disconnected regions.

Each player has fourteen pieces in two ranks of seven, centred on their own side, leaving two squares of margin at each end so the corners stay empty:

In a two-player game Amber takes the south side and Violet the north. In a four-player game Jade takes the west and Coral the east, and turns pass Amber → Jade → Violet → Coral. Amber always moves first.

2. Turns

On your turn you take exactly one action: a move to an empty square, an attack on an enemy piece, a strike against an enemy barrier, or placing your barrier. Passing is not allowed. No piece may move onto, or slide through, a void, an occupied square or a barrier.

2b. The move clock

Each turn is capped at a time limit — one minute by default. Run out and you forfeit that turn: the board is untouched, you keep every piece, and play simply passes to the next player. You are not eliminated and you get your normal turn again next time round.

The limit is chosen in Settings, and in an online game the host's setting governs the table so everyone counts down together. Set it to "No limit" to switch the clock off. Because a forfeited turn leaves the position unchanged, two players idling in turn will repeat the position and the game will end by repetition — a game where nobody is present ends itself.

The host is the only one that may declare a timeout, and it tells everyone else. Other players run the same countdown for display, so a slow connection can never make two people disagree about whose turn it is.

3. Voids

A void is a permanent hole. Any piece displaced onto a void is removed from the game. Voids never fill up. They block movement and push paths, but they do not block pull scans.

4. Pushing

A push scans outward from the attacker in one direction. Every square between the attacker and its target must be empty; a void, a barrier or any piece stops the scan short. The first enemy piece found within range is the target.

The chain is the target plus any pieces standing in an unbroken line directly behind it, up to two pieces in total. Three or more stacked in the push direction makes the push illegal.

The chain is driven n squares away, one step at a time. On each step:

  • If the square ahead of the leading piece is off the board or holds a barrier, the push stops there and the remaining steps are forfeited.
  • If it is a void, the leading piece falls in and is removed, and everything behind advances one square into the space freed up.
  • Otherwise every piece in the chain advances one square.

If nothing can move even one square, the push is not a legal move. Otherwise the attacker finishes by advancing into the square the target originally occupied — one square for a contact attacker, two for the Spear.

Consequence worth noting: a two-piece chain driven two squares into a hole directly ahead of it loses both pieces, because the second step feeds the survivor into the same void.

5. Pulling

A pull scans outward, passing over both empty squares and voids but never over a barrier. The first piece the scan meets is the target. If that piece is your own, or is directly adjacent to you, there is no pull.

The target is dragged one square toward the attacker. That square is necessarily empty or a void; if it is a void, the target is removed. The puller does not move, and pulls never chain.

6. Barriers

Each player has exactly one barrier, held off the board at the start. Placing it costs a whole turn and it may go on any empty, non-void square anywhere on the board. It cannot be moved, pushed or pulled afterwards.

A barrier blocks everything, including its owner's own pieces: no movement onto or through it, no push path, no pull scan. It starts at 3 health.

Any piece whose push or pull scan is stopped by a barrier it does not own may spend its turn striking it instead, removing 1 health. The striker does not move. Range and direction follow that piece's normal attack geometry — a Sword must be adjacent, a Spear must be exactly two squares away, a Rod or Bow can strike from any distance along its line, and a puller cannot strike an adjacent barrier for the same reason it cannot pull an adjacent piece. At 0 health the barrier is removed and the square becomes ordinary ground. You may never strike your own barrier.

8. Elimination and winning

  1. Trident. A player whose Trident is removed is out immediately. Their remaining pieces and barrier stay on the board as neutral: they never take a turn, but they still block, can still be pushed and pulled by anyone, and can still be dropped into holes. In the rare case where a single push removes the last two Tridents at once, the player who made that move loses.
  2. Last standing. When only one player still has a Trident, they win.
  3. Repetition. When the same position — the same pieces and barriers on the same squares with the same player to move — occurs for the third time, the game ends and the surviving player with the most pieces wins. An exact tie is a draw.
  4. Running out of time. Forfeits that turn only — never the game, and never a piece.
  5. No legal moves. A player with no legal action on their turn is out. In a two-player game that ends it; with more players the game continues without them.
  6. Resignation and disconnection. Both remove that player's Trident and follow the elimination rule above.

9. Notation

Moves are recorded as a piece letter, the origin square, a connector, and the target square. Because Sword, Scythe and Spear all begin with the same letter, the letters are Sword, sCythe, sPear, Bow, Rod, Trident.

A turn lost to the clock is recorded as …. - is a quiet move, > a push, < a pull, ! a barrier strike. A trailing digit is a push distance greater than one; each * is a piece that went into a void. Placing a barrier is + and the square.

So Rc3<c5* is a Rod pulling the piece on c5 into a hole, Pc2>c4 2** is a Spear driving a pair two squares and losing both to the same void, +f6 drops a barrier on f6, and Sf4!f5(2) hits a barrier down to two health.

About

Undertow is an original two- or four-player abstract strategy game. This is the complete implementation — engine, interface and networking — running entirely in your browser.

How Quick Match works without a server

Room codes are easy: the broker only has to answer "where is the peer named ABC23?". Matchmaking asks a harder question — "who else is waiting right now?" — and something normally has to remember that list.

Instead, players agree on a well-known name. Looking for a game, your browser first tries to connect to that name. If somebody answers, you're matched. If nobody does, you become that name and wait.

A waiting player can't simply play on the well-known name — releasing it later would kill the game, and holding it would block everyone else. So the waiter runs a throwaway beacon under that name alongside its own private connection. The beacon hands each arrival the private address and, once the table is full, shuts down and frees the name for the next group.

It is honest about its limits: it works well when a handful of people are online and gets slower as more pile in, there's no queue or ordering, and a browser that dies leaves a stale name that others waste a few seconds on. Room codes are always there as the reliable path.

Chat

Every online arena has chat, available from the moment you start waiting for players and carried through into the game. Messages travel the same peer-to-peer path as moves — nothing is stored anywhere, and the log is gone when you leave.

Guests send only the text of a message. The host stamps the seat it arrived on, so nobody can post as another player, and every message is inserted into the page as text rather than markup, so a message containing HTML shows up as the characters you typed.

Nobody trusts anybody

The host is authoritative. Guests send an intent — a short reference to a move, never a board state — and the host regenerates the full list of legal moves for the current position and looks for a match before broadcasting the result. Every other player re-derives it the same way. A move that isn't in the list is rejected, so a modified client cannot force an illegal position.

Design notes

The rule that shapes everything else is that pushes always end with the attacker taking the square its target vacated. That makes a push simultaneously an attack and an advance.

The second is that pull scans ignore voids while push scans are blocked by them. Holes are cover against half the army and an open lane for the other half, so the same square is safe or lethal depending on what is looking at it.

The barrier is the counterweight to both. It is the only thing that stops a pull, which means it's the only reliable answer to a Rod that has found a good hole.

Playing offline

Everything except online play works with no internet connection at all — the rules, the board, the settings and the themes are entirely local. Only the initial handshake between players needs the network.

Verifying the rules

The rule engine has its own test suite covering push chains, wall blocks, void and barrier interactions, the pull-across-a-hole case, four-player elimination, every ending, notation, network move validation, and randomised full-game fuzz runs for both two and four players. Open tests.html to run it.
